## A Wound That Would Not Heal

The defeat at Badr, which we examined in detail across the previous unit, was not simply a military setback that Quraysh could absorb and move past through ordinary strategic recalibration. It struck at something deeper and more personal: the honor of a tribe that had, for generations, considered itself the unquestioned power of the Arabian Peninsula.

Senior and respected leaders had been killed — among them Abu Jahl, whose opposition to the Prophet's ﷺ mission we traced across multiple lessons in Section 4. The manner of the defeat compounded the wound: a force of roughly a thousand men, equipped and confident, had been routed by a much smaller group of just over three hundred. This was not a loss that could be quietly absorbed. It demanded, in the logic of Arabian tribal honor that we studied extensively in earlier sections, a response.

## Hind's Grief and Its Direction

Among those whose grief following Badr would shape the events ahead most directly was Hind bint Utbah, wife of Abu Sufyan and a woman of considerable standing within Quraysh.

She had lost her father Utbah, her uncle Shaybah, and her brother al-Walid — all three killed in the single combat that opened the Battle of Badr, which we examined in detail in the previous unit. Her grief did not remain private. She channeled it into public advocacy for revenge, composing and reciting poetry intended to stir the men of Quraysh toward renewed war, and ultimately resolved to accompany the army herself when it marched toward Medina.

Her presence at the campaign, and the specific actions she would take during and after the battle, form one of the more difficult chapters we will examine later in this unit — difficult, but necessary to address honestly as part of a complete and truthful account of what occurred at Uhud.

## The Mobilization

Over the course of the year following Badr, Quraysh worked methodically to assemble a force considerably larger and more determined than what they had brought the previous time. This effort drew not only on Quraysh's own resources but on alliances and contributions from across their broader network of tribal relationships.

The result was an army of approximately three thousand men — nearly triple the size of the force defeated at Badr — equipped with cavalry and supplies for a sustained campaign, reflecting the seriousness with which Quraysh now approached the conflict. This was no longer a matter of protecting a caravan that had happened to attract Muslim attention. This was a deliberate, well-resourced campaign aimed directly at Medina itself, intended to settle the matter decisively in Quraysh's favor.

## Word Reaches Medina

As this mobilization proceeded, the Prophet ﷺ received word of its scale and intentions through his uncle Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib.

We encountered Abbas previously in Section 4, presiding over the second pledge of Aqabah despite not yet having formally accepted Islam himself — a man whose personal loyalty to his nephew consistently exceeded any strict requirement of shared religious conviction. He remained in Mecca following the Hijrah but maintained sympathetic contact with the Prophet ﷺ, and when Quraysh's mobilization reached a scale that made their intentions unmistakable, he sent word ahead to Medina.

This advance warning proved valuable. It gave the Prophet ﷺ and the Muslim community time to consider their strategic options carefully, rather than being caught entirely unprepared by an army of this size approaching their city. The nature of that consideration — and the significant disagreement it produced within the Muslim community regarding the best strategy to adopt — is the subject of our next lesson.

## What Lay Ahead

As Quraysh's army began its march northward toward Medina, the Muslim community faced a fundamentally different strategic situation than they had encountered before Badr. At Badr, the confrontation had emerged somewhat unexpectedly from what began as a caravan-monitoring mission. Here, the threat was direct, deliberate, and aimed explicitly at the heart of the Muslim community's home territory.

The question facing the Prophet ﷺ and his companions رضي الله عنهم was not whether to defend themselves — that much was settled by the very nature of the threat approaching. The question was how: whether to remain within Medina's defensible structures and let the city's natural and constructed protections work in their favor, or to march out and meet Quraysh's army in open battle before it could reach the city itself.

This question, and the council that debated it, would shape everything that followed — including, as we will see, a costly disagreement whose consequences extended well beyond the battle itself.
