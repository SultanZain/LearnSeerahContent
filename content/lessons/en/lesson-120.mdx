## A Remarkable Instruction Given in Advance

Before the expedition to Mu'tah departed in response to the killing of the Prophet's ﷺ messenger, examined in the previous lesson, he gave an instruction of genuine and remarkable specificity regarding the chain of command that would govern this particular mission.

He named, in explicit order, three commanders who would successively lead the expedition should each preceding leader fall in the engagement ahead: first Zayd ibn Harithah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, the freed slave and adopted son whose early acceptance of Islam we examined in Section 4; then Ja'far ibn Abi Talib <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, whose eloquent and moving speech before the Negus we studied in such detail in that same section; and finally Abdullah ibn Rawahah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, a companion رضي الله عنه known throughout the Muslim community for his poetry and his sincere devotion.

## An Overwhelming Force Encountered

As the Muslim expedition of approximately three thousand men advanced toward the region of Mu'tah, they encountered a force of a scale that presented one of the most severe strategic challenges the Muslim community had yet faced in any engagement examined throughout this curriculum.

Various historical accounts describe the combined Byzantine and allied Arab forces reaching numbers well beyond one hundred thousand — a disparity considerably exceeding even the significant numerical disadvantages we examined at Badr in Unit 15 and Uhud in Unit 17. This was, by any conventional measure, an engagement the Muslim expedition had little realistic prospect of winning through direct confrontation alone.

## Three Commanders Fall in Succession

The battle unfolded with a costly and moving succession of leadership exactly as the Prophet's ﷺ advance instruction had anticipated might become necessary.

Zayd ibn Harithah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, leading the Muslim forces, was killed in the fighting. Ja'far ibn Abi Talib <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> took up the banner as the Prophet's ﷺ instruction had specified, and he fought with an intensity and courage that has become one of the most celebrated individual accounts of martyrdom in the entire history of early Islam.

The narrations describe Ja'far <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> continuing to fight even after his right arm was severed in the battle, transferring the banner to his left hand and continuing forward. When his left arm was subsequently severed as well, he is described as holding the banner between the stumps of his remaining arms, refusing to allow it to fall, until he was finally killed in the fighting.

## Wings in Jannah

The Prophet ﷺ, remaining in Medina at the time of this battle, received news of these events through revelation before any physical messenger could possibly have reached him with word of what had occurred at such a considerable distance.

He described to those around him a vision he had received of Ja'far <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> in Jannah, flying with two wings given to him by Allah in place of the two arms he had sacrificed in the course of his faithful service and martyrdom. This vision gave rise to the enduring title by which Ja'far <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> has been known throughout the subsequent history of Islam: Ja'far al-Tayyar, Ja'far the Flier — a title connecting directly back to the eloquent and courageous man whose speech before the Negus we examined with such admiration in Section 4, and whose life of service to the truth found its final and most complete expression in this moment of ultimate sacrifice.

## Abdullah ibn Rawahah رضي الله عنه and the Turn to Khalid

Following Ja'far's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> martyrdom, Abdullah ibn Rawahah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> took up the banner as the third commander the Prophet's ﷺ instruction had named, and he too was killed in the continuing engagement, having fought with the same devotion and courage as his two predecessors.

With all three commanders named in advance now fallen, the remaining Muslim soldiers, facing an overwhelming and increasingly desperate situation, turned to Khalid ibn al-Walid — the very commander whose tactical skill in exploiting the archers' departure from their position at Uhud we examined in Unit 17, and who had, in the intervening period, accepted Islam himself.

## A Skillful Withdrawal, Not a Decisive Victory

Khalid <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, now leading the remaining Muslim forces, did not achieve a decisive military victory over the vastly larger Byzantine and allied army they faced — a realistic outcome would not have permitted such an achievement given the severity of the numerical disadvantage involved. What he did achieve, through skillful and creative tactical maneuvering, was an organized and effective withdrawal that preserved the majority of the Muslim expedition, avoiding the kind of complete and catastrophic destruction that might otherwise have resulted from continued direct confrontation against such overwhelming numbers.

This outcome, while not a triumphant victory in the conventional sense, represented a genuine achievement given the circumstances — the preservation of a significant portion of the Muslim community's military capacity in the face of what could easily have become a total and devastating defeat.

## What Mu'tah Represents

The Battle of Mu'tah stands as a significant milestone in the broader history of this period — the first major military confrontation between the growing Muslim community and the forces of the Byzantine Empire, one of the two great powers whose diplomatic reception we examined in detail throughout Unit 24. It also stands as one of the most moving accounts of individual sacrifice and devotion preserved from the entire early history of Islam, through the successive martyrdoms of Zayd, Ja'far, and Abdullah ibn Rawahah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنهم</span>.

We turn in our next and final lesson of this unit to the broader consequences of this period, examining how Quraysh's own subsequent breach of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah would ultimately set the stage for the conquest of Mecca itself.
