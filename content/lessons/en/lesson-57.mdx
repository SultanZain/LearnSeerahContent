## A Question Without an Easy Answer

The mosque had been built. The community had grown. Five daily prayers were now an established part of Muslim life, following the command received at the Sidrat al-Muntaha during the Mi'raj.

But a practical question remained, one that might seem small until you consider how essential its answer would become: how does an entire community know when it is time to gather for prayer?

There was no broadcast technology, no synchronized clocks, no simple way to alert hundreds of people scattered across homes and date palm gardens that the moment for Dhuhr or Asr or Maghrib had arrived.

The Prophet ﷺ gathered his companions رضي الله عنهم and asked for their thoughts.

## What They Considered and Set Aside

Several suggestions came forward, each reflecting practices the companions رضي الله عنهم had encountered among the other religious communities of Arabia.

Some suggested a bell, similar to what was used by Christian communities to call people to gather. Others suggested a horn, similar to the trumpet-like instrument used by Jewish communities for certain occasions. Someone suggested simply lighting a fire that could be seen from a distance as a visual signal.

None of these felt right. Each option carried an association with a different religious community, and there was a sense — though not yet a clear solution — that the new faith needed something of its own, something that expressed what Islam actually was rather than borrowing the external form of another tradition.

The matter was left unresolved. Until a dream changed everything.

## A Dream in the Night

Abdullah ibn Zayd <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, one of the Ansar, came to the Prophet ﷺ the next morning with something he had seen while he slept.

He described a man passing by him, carrying a bell. Abdullah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, in the dream, asked to buy it — thinking, even in his sleep, of the unresolved question facing the community.

*"What will you do with it?"* the man asked.

*"We will call people to prayer with it."*

*"Shall I not show you something better than that?"*

*"Yes."*

And the man taught him, in the dream, the words that would become the Adhan — phrase by phrase, building from the declaration of Allah's greatness through the testimony of faith, to the call to prayer and to success, and back again to the declaration of Allah's oneness.

Abdullah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> woke and went immediately to the Prophet ﷺ to tell him what he had seen.

## Confirmed and Given Voice

The Prophet ﷺ listened to Abdullah's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> account and confirmed it: *"This is a true vision, if Allah wills."*

He instructed Abdullah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> to teach the words to Bilal ibn Rabah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — the freed slave whose story of steadfastness under torture we studied in Section 4 — because Bilal's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> voice was stronger and carried further across the settlement.

Bilal <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> climbed to a high point near the mosque and gave the call for the very first time in human history.

His voice — the same voice that had said *Ahad, Ahad* through hours of torture on the burning sands of Mecca — now rose over the rooftops of Medina, calling the believers to prayer.

There is a particular fittingness in this. The man who had once been told that his voice, his very breath, would be the price of his suffering if he did not renounce his faith — that same voice was now the instrument through which an entire community would be summoned to worship Allah, five times a day, for the rest of history.

## Umar's رضي الله عنه Confirmation

When Umar ibn al-Khattab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> heard Bilal's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> call ringing out for the first time, he came running to the Prophet ﷺ in a state of astonishment.

*"By the One who sent you with the truth — I saw exactly what he saw, word for word. But I was too shy to mention it before he did."*

The Prophet ﷺ responded: *"Then praise be to Allah."*

Two companions رضي الله عنهم, on the same night, had been shown the same vision. This was treated by the community not as a curious coincidence but as a confirmation — Allah giving the same guidance through two separate witnesses, removing any doubt about the authenticity of what had been received.

## What the Words Actually Say

It is worth pausing to consider the actual content of the Adhan, since its familiarity can sometimes obscure how much theological weight it carries in a remarkably short span of words.

*Allahu Akbar* — Allah is greater. Repeated at the opening, establishing immediately that whatever is about to be said, whatever the listener is about to be called toward, exists under the absolute supremacy of Allah over all things.

*Ash-hadu an la ilaha illa Allah* — I bear witness that there is no god but Allah. The core declaration of Tawhid that we studied at length in Section 1, proclaimed publicly, multiple times daily.

*Ash-hadu anna Muhammadan rasul Allah* — I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah. The seal of Prophethood, declared in the same breath as the oneness of Allah.

*Hayya 'ala al-salah* — come to prayer. *Hayya 'ala al-falah* — come to success. The call frames prayer not as an obligation to be endured but as the path to genuine success, using the same word, falah, that the Quran uses repeatedly to describe the ultimate flourishing of the believer.

In the span of less than a minute, repeated five times a day, every Muslim community on earth proclaims publicly the entire core of the Islamic message. This is what Abdullah ibn Zayd <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> was shown in his sleep, and what Bilal's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> voice first carried across the rooftops of Medina — a brief, beautiful, complete declaration of faith, given to the world not through a council or a committee, but through a dream confirmed by revelation, and a voice that had already proven, on the burning sands of Mecca, exactly what it was capable of carrying.
