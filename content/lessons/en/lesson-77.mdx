## A Grief Commissioned in Advance

We began this unit by examining how Hind bint Utbah's losses at Badr — her father Utbah, her uncle Shaybah, and her brother al-Walid, all killed in the single combat that opened that battle — transformed into a determined public advocacy for revenge that brought her to the battlefield at Uhud itself.

That grief found its specific target during the chaos following the rear attack we examined in the previous lesson. Hind had specifically commissioned Wahshi ibn Harb, a man skilled with the javelin, with a precise task: find and kill Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, whose role in the single combat at Badr had directly resulted in the death of her uncle Shaybah.

## The Lion Falls

Hamzah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> had fought throughout the battle with the same fearless intensity that had defined his entire character since the moment we examined his transformation from tribal honor into genuine faith earlier in this section. Amid the confusion of the rear attack, Wahshi found his opportunity, throwing his javelin and striking Hamzah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> with a fatal blow.

The man whose conversion had begun with a bow raised in fury at an insult to his nephew, who had fought at Badr with a ferocity the entire battlefield remembered, fell at Uhud — earning, through this death, the title that has belonged to him in Islamic memory ever since: Asadullah, the Lion of Allah.

## What Happened to His Body

What followed requires this curriculum to address something genuinely difficult, and it does so without hesitation or omission, because honesty about history — including its most painful moments — is essential to teaching the Seerah with integrity.

Hind bint Utbah, upon learning of Hamzah's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> death, came to his body and, in an act of extreme cruelty driven by the depth of her grief and her desire for vengeance, mutilated his remains.

This was a genuine wrong, and this curriculum names it as such without softening or minimizing what occurred. It was an act of tyranny and cruelty that caused profound additional pain to the Prophet ﷺ and the entire Muslim community, compounding the already significant loss of one of their most beloved and courageous members.

## The Prophet's ﷺ Grief

When the Prophet ﷺ came upon his uncle's body and discovered what had been done, he wept — and the narrations describe this as a grief of unusual depth, even among the many losses the Prophet ﷺ would experience throughout his life.

> *"Never will I be struck by a calamity like this one."*

This response confirms something we observed in the previous unit regarding the martyrs of Badr: genuine certainty about the reward awaiting the martyr in the next life does not require a believer, even a Prophet, to suppress the real and appropriate grief that accompanies losing someone deeply loved. The Prophet ﷺ mourned his uncle visibly and completely, and this mourning stands as a model for how Muslims throughout history have understood the appropriate balance between hope in Allah's mercy and the honest expression of human grief.

## What Came After — And Why It Matters Completely

This is the point in the story where this curriculum must speak with particular care and precision, because what happened afterward changes entirely how this episode should be remembered and discussed.

Years later, following the conquest of Mecca that we will study in a later unit, Hind bint Utbah came to accept Islam. She came before the Prophet ﷺ, sincerely embraced the faith, and from that point forward lived as a believer — by the accounts of the Seerah, living the remainder of her life with genuine devotion to the religion she had once fought against with such determination.

The Prophet ﷺ accepted her acceptance of Islam completely, exactly as he accepted any other sincere convert, without condition and without any continuing punishment or distinction held against her for what she had done before her conversion. Allah's mercy, and the Prophet's ﷺ forgiveness extended on Allah's behalf, did not carry an asterisk or an exception for the severity of her earlier wrong.

## A Principle This Curriculum Holds Firmly

This matters for a reason that extends beyond simply completing her biographical account. The cruelty she inflicted on Hamzah's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> body was real, and it was tyranny — this curriculum does not soften that fact in the slightest. But that cruelty belonged to a person before her acceptance of Islam, and the wrong it represents was a wrong against Hamzah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> specifically, the one person with the standing to be wronged by it.

Once the Prophet ﷺ — who held every right and every reason to respond otherwise — chose complete forgiveness, that forgiveness settled the matter for the entire Ummah going forward. A believer studying this history is not in a position to hold a grudge or curse a person whom the Prophet ﷺ himself, with full knowledge of exactly what she had done to his beloved uncle, chose to forgive entirely.

This is not a small point of historical trivia. It reflects something central to Islamic teaching about repentance, mercy, and the limits of what believers are permitted to carry forward in judgment against someone Allah and His Messenger ﷺ have already forgiven. The cruelty is named honestly. The forgiveness is honored completely. Both truths stand together, exactly as the Prophet's ﷺ own example requires.
