## A Clear and Necessary Response

Having discovered the specific and calculated plot against his life that we examined in detail in the previous lesson, the Prophet ﷺ moved to address the situation directly. Given the severity of what Banu al-Nadir had attempted — a premeditated assassination plot against the community's own designated arbiter, undertaken during a meeting convened for peaceful dispute resolution — a response was clearly required.

He issued an ultimatum: Banu al-Nadir had ten days to leave Medina. They would be permitted to take their movable possessions with them, but remaining beyond this period would not be accepted given the gravity of what had occurred.

## Abdullah ibn Ubayy's Familiar Pattern

At this point, a figure whose behavior we have now examined at considerable length across previous lessons reappears in this narrative. Abdullah ibn Ubayy, whose resentment toward the growing authority of the Prophet ﷺ we traced back to his lost aspirations for kingship in Unit 16, and whose damaging withdrawal during the march to Uhud we examined in detail in the previous unit, once again involved himself in a manner calculated to undermine the stability the Constitution of Medina had been designed to protect.

He sent word to Banu al-Nadir encouraging them to defy the ultimatum, fortify their position, and resist rather than comply — accompanied by a promise that he and his allied supporters would come to their military aid if the Muslims moved to enforce the ultimatum through force.

This intervention proved decisive in shaping Banu al-Nadir's initial response. Rather than accepting the terms of the ultimatum and departing peacefully as Banu Qaynuqa had ultimately done, they chose instead to fortify their position and prepare for confrontation, relying on the military support Abdullah ibn Ubayy had promised.

## A Siege and an Unfulfilled Promise

The Prophet ﷺ led a force to besiege Banu al-Nadir's now-fortified position, consistent with the response required when a signatory community chose open defiance over the peaceful resolution the Constitution had established as the appropriate channel for any grievance.

As the siege proceeded, Banu al-Nadir waited for the military support Abdullah ibn Ubayy had promised them. It never arrived.

This pattern should feel familiar by this point in our study, connecting directly to what we examined regarding Abdullah ibn Ubayy's conduct during the march to Uhud — a consistent pattern of encouraging resistance or making promises of support that, when the moment of actual test arrived, failed to materialize into genuine action. Whether this reflects a lack of genuine capacity to follow through, a fundamental unseriousness in the original promise, or simple unwillingness to bear the actual cost and risk of confrontation once it became real rather than hypothetical, the effect on Banu al-Nadir was the same: they found themselves isolated, having chosen defiance based on a support system that proved illusory precisely when they needed it most.

## Departure Without the Support They Expected

After approximately two weeks of siege, and with the promised assistance from Abdullah ibn Ubayy clearly not forthcoming, Banu al-Nadir recognized the futility of continued resistance. They agreed to the terms of the original ultimatum, departing Medina with the movable possessions they were permitted to take.

Their departure took them primarily toward Khaybar, an oasis settlement to the north that we will return to examine in detail in a later unit of this section, where a significant Jewish community had already established itself and where Banu al-Nadir would resettle and, in time, become entangled in further conflicts with the growing Muslim community of Medina.

## Comparing the Two Expulsions

Placing this outcome alongside what we examined regarding Banu Qaynuqa in Unit 16 reveals both continuity and important distinction in how the Prophet ﷺ handled breaches of the Constitution by different communities under different circumstances.

Both situations ultimately resulted in expulsion rather than the harsher consequences available under the customs of warfare in that era — a consistent pattern of proportionate and measured response rather than uniform severity applied without regard to circumstance. But the paths toward that shared outcome differed meaningfully. Banu Qaynuqa's situation arose from an escalating marketplace dispute and benefited from Abdullah ibn Ubayy's successful intervention on their behalf. Banu al-Nadir's situation arose from a calculated assassination plot, a qualitatively more severe breach, and this time Abdullah ibn Ubayy's involvement — encouraging resistance rather than urging compliance — produced no benefit for the tribe at all, since his promised support simply never came.

## What This Pattern Establishes

Taken together, these two episodes demonstrate that the Constitution of Medina, examined in Section 5 and tested repeatedly throughout this section, continued to function as a genuine and enforceable political order — one capable of accommodating varying circumstances and responding proportionately to the specific severity of different breaches, while consistently avoiding the harshest available consequences even when the underlying violation, as in the case of a premeditated assassination attempt, was severe indeed.

This pattern of measured, proportionate response matters considerably for understanding what comes next in our study of this period. As we move forward into the growing coalition of forces that would soon converge on Medina — a threat considerably larger and more coordinated than anything we have examined thus far — the question of how the Muslim community would respond to internal threats within its own walls, including the treatment of Banu Qurayza during the crisis of the Trench, deserves to be considered against this established backdrop of consistent proportionality rather than assumed severity.

We turn next to the immediate concern that this expulsion helped set into motion: the beginning of a much larger and more dangerous alliance against Medina, one that would bring together Quraysh, several allied tribes, and eventually elements connected to the very communities we have examined in this unit, in what would become known as the Battle of the Trench.
