## A Marketplace and an Insult

Among the three major Jewish tribes of Medina that we studied as signatories to the Constitution in Section 5, Banu Qaynuqa held a particular position as skilled goldsmiths and craftsmen, with their primary marketplace serving as an important commercial center for the city.

It was in this very marketplace that the incident occurred which would set in motion the first significant breach of the Constitution's terms by any of its signatory communities.

A Muslim woman had come to the market to conduct business with a goldsmith of Banu Qaynuqa. As a cruel prank, the goldsmith arranged her garment in a way that would expose her when she stood, then pinned it without her knowledge. When she rose, the resulting exposure caused a public humiliation that drew laughter and mockery from those present.

## From Insult to Killing

A Muslim man who witnessed this responded immediately, killing the goldsmith responsible for the cruel arrangement.

This, in turn, prompted members of Banu Qaynuqa to retaliate by killing the Muslim man. What had begun as a single act of personal cruelty in a marketplace had now escalated into a cycle of violence between members of the Muslim community and members of Banu Qaynuqa — precisely the kind of dispute the Constitution of Medina had been designed to channel toward peaceful resolution rather than uncontrolled escalation.

## A Pattern of Broader Tension

This marketplace incident, while serious in itself, did not occur in isolation. The narrations of the Seerah describe a broader pattern of growing tension between Banu Qaynuqa and the Muslim community in the period following the victory at Badr.

Some accounts describe members of the tribe reacting to the Muslim victory at Badr with open hostility and provocation, including taunts suggesting that the Muslims should not assume their success against Quraysh would translate to similar success if Banu Qaynuqa chose to oppose them — a direct challenge to the security arrangements the Constitution had established.

Rather than pursuing resolution through the dispute mechanisms the Constitution provided — which, as we studied in Section 5, specifically designated the Prophet ﷺ as the arbiter for disagreements between the city's communities — Banu Qaynuqa withdrew into their fortified quarter, a clear signal of open defiance rather than continued participation in the shared political order of Medina.

## The Siege and Its Resolution

The Prophet ﷺ responded by leading a force to besiege the fortified position of Banu Qaynuqa. The siege lasted approximately two weeks before the tribe, recognizing the futility of continued resistance, surrendered.

At this point, a significant intervention occurred. Abdullah ibn Ubayy — whose complicated history of resentment and partial allegiance we examined in detail in the previous lesson — had long-standing alliance relationships with Banu Qaynuqa from before the arrival of Islam in Medina. He came to the Prophet ﷺ and pleaded urgently on their behalf, asking for leniency.

The narrations describe this as a moment of considerable tension, with Abdullah ibn Ubayy reportedly grasping the Prophet's ﷺ garment and pressing his appeal with unusual insistence. The Prophet ﷺ, after this intercession, agreed to a comparatively lenient resolution: rather than the more severe consequences that the customs of warfare in that era and region would have permitted following an open breach of this kind, Banu Qaynuqa were allowed to leave Medina peacefully, taking their movable possessions with them.

## Why This Comparison Matters

It is worth pausing here to note something that will become significant as we proceed through the units ahead, particularly when we reach the events surrounding Banu al-Nadir and the far more serious situation with Banu Qurayza during the Battle of the Trench.

The outcome for Banu Qaynuqa — expulsion with their property, following a process that included siege, surrender, and external intercession — represents a notably measured response given the seriousness of an open breach of the Constitution's security provisions. This pattern of escalating consequences for escalating breaches, rather than a uniform or predetermined harsh response applied regardless of circumstance, is important to keep in mind as we examine the more severe outcomes that would follow in later, more serious incidents.

Each situation, as we will see, was judged according to its own specific circumstances — the nature and severity of the breach, the conduct of the parties involved, and the broader security context the Muslim community faced at that particular moment. Banu Qaynuqa's situation, serious as it was, did not yet involve the kind of coordinated military threat to the very survival of Medina that later episodes would present.

## What the Constitution's Enforcement Demonstrates

Returning to the framework we established in Section 5: the Constitution of Medina was not a ceremonial document with no real teeth, nor was it a one-sided arrangement granting Muslims unlimited authority over the city's other communities regardless of their own conduct.

It was a functioning political and legal order, with the Prophet ﷺ serving as the agreed-upon arbiter for disputes, and with real consequences attached to genuine breaches of its terms — consequences that, as we have seen here, could be tempered by appeal, intercession, and the Prophet's ﷺ own inclination toward the more measured available response rather than the harshest one possible.

## A First Test, Not a Final Word

The Banu Qaynuqa episode represents the first significant test of whether the pluralistic political order established by the Constitution could actually function when one of its signatory communities chose confrontation over the peaceful resolution mechanisms the document provided.

The relatively measured response — siege, surrender, expulsion with property rather than harsher alternatives — demonstrates a system working roughly as designed, even under real strain. As we will see in the units ahead, the security situation facing Medina would grow considerably more severe in the years that followed, and the consequences for subsequent breaches by other communities within the city would, correspondingly, reflect that growing severity.

For now, we turn to a different and more specifically targeted internal threat: the case of Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, a poet whose words were used as weapons of war in a manner that required the Prophet ﷺ to respond to an entirely different category of danger.
