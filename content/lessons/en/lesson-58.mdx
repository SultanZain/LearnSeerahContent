## Thirteen Years Without Retaliation

It is worth pausing, before we examine the permission to fight, to remember exactly what preceded it.

For thirteen years in Mecca, the Muslims endured persecution that we studied in detail across Section 4 — mockery, economic boycott, physical torture, assassination plots, and the deaths of believers like Sumayyah and Yasir <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنهما</span>. And throughout all of it, not a single act of armed retaliation was authorized or carried out.

This is remarkable when you consider who was among the believers. Hamzah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — a man feared across Mecca for his physical strength. Umar <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — a man whose directness and force of will made him one of the most formidable personalities in the city. Men entirely capable of meeting violence with violence chose, under the Prophet's ﷺ guidance, to endure instead.

This was not weakness. It was discipline, rooted in the absence of the conditions that would make armed conflict a just and structured response rather than chaotic retaliation. Those conditions arrived only after the Hijrah, with the establishment of an organized Muslim community possessing its own territory, its own governance, and its own collective responsibility for self-defense.

## The First Permission

The Quran marks the shift with a verse whose wording leaves little room for misunderstanding about its purpose:

<Quran surah="22" ayah="39-40" />

*Permission has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged. And indeed, Allah is competent to give them victory. Those who have been evicted from their homes without right — only because they say, "Our Lord is Allah."*

Notice the structure of the verse. It does not say permission is given to expand territory, to compel belief, or to settle old scores. It says permission is given to those who have been wronged — who have been driven from their own homes, for no reason except declaring their faith in one God.

This is the language of defense against persecution, stated as plainly as language allows.

## A Reason That Reaches Beyond Islam Alone

The same passage continues with a justification that extends its scope beyond the Muslim community specifically:

<Quran surah="22" ayah="40" />

*And were it not that Allah checks the people, some by means of others, there would have been demolished monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which the name of Allah is much mentioned.*

This is a striking statement to find at the foundation of the permission to fight. The Quran does not frame the value being protected as Islam's dominance alone. It names four distinct categories of religious institution — Christian monasteries, Christian churches, Jewish synagogues, and Muslim mosques — and identifies the destruction of any of them as a loss worth preventing.

The underlying principle is that a world with no check on aggression, no mechanism for the wronged to defend themselves, would eventually see every house of worship destroyed by whichever power happened to dominate at a given moment. Permitted, regulated self-defense becomes the mechanism by which religious freedom itself — for every faith, not Islam exclusively — is preserved against unchecked power.

## Rules Where There Had Been None

Once fighting became permissible, the Prophet ﷺ did something that distinguished Islamic warfare from the unrestrained tribal conflicts of the Jahiliyyah we studied in Section 4: he established clear ethical limits.

Before sending out any military expedition, the Prophet ﷺ would instruct the commander with guidance that became the consistent standard for the Muslim armies:

> *"Do not kill a child, a woman, or an aged, infirm person... Do not kill the people who are sitting in places of worship."*
> — Sunan Abi Dawud, 2614

The instructions extended further: do not destroy fruit-bearing trees, do not slaughter animals except for food, do not betray a trust, and do not mutilate the bodies of those who have fallen in battle.

These were not minor technicalities. In an Arabia where tribal warfare had traditionally meant the destruction of an enemy's entire capacity to survive — crops burned, wells poisoned, women and children killed alongside warriors, the dead mutilated as trophies — the introduction of these limits represented a fundamental reordering of what conflict was permitted to look like.

## A Permission, Not a Command to Aggress

It is essential, as we move into the chapters ahead that describe the battles of Badr, Uhud, and the others, to hold this foundation clearly in mind.

The permission to fight that came in Medina was exactly that — a permission, granted under specific conditions, bound by specific ethical rules, and justified explicitly by the persecution the Muslims had already endured for over a decade without retaliating.

It was not an open license for conquest. It was not a command to seek out and attack anyone who had not accepted Islam. The battles we are about to study — Badr in particular — were fought in circumstances where Quraysh itself marched out to attack the Muslim community, not the reverse.

Understanding this sequence matters because the history of the Madinan period is sometimes presented, by those unfamiliar with its actual context, as though the peaceful Prophet ﷺ of Mecca suddenly became a different person upon reaching Medina. The truth is more precise and more coherent: the same man, with the same character, the same patience, and the same commitment to justice, was now responsible for the survival and protection of an actual community — and Allah granted him, at exactly the moment that responsibility became real, the permission needed to protect it within carefully defined limits.

The battles ahead were not chosen. In nearly every case, as we will see, they were brought to the doorstep of Medina by those who had already spent thirteen years trying to extinguish this message by every means available to them.
