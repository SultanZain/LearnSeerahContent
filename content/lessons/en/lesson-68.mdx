## A King Who Never Was

To understand Abdullah ibn Ubayy, you have to understand what he lost — not in the sense of property or wealth, but in the sense of a future that had nearly been his and then dissolved entirely.

We studied in Section 5 how the Aws and Khazraj had spent generations locked in a devastating cycle of conflict, culminating in the particularly bloody Battle of Bu'ath. By the time that exhaustion had run its course, the leading men of Medina had begun moving toward a solution that Arabian tribal politics occasionally produced in moments of crisis: unifying both tribes under a single ruler whose authority both sides would accept.

Abdullah ibn Ubayy was that man. The narrations describe preparations already underway — a crown reportedly being fashioned for the ceremony that would have formalized his kingship over the city.

And then the message of Islam arrived, carried first by six men at al-Aqabah, then taught patiently across a year by Musab ibn Umayr <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, until both tribes found themselves united not around a single tribal king, but around submission to Allah and allegiance to a Prophet who had not even set foot in their city yet.

## What This Created

It is not difficult to imagine the particular quality of resentment this situation produced. Abdullah ibn Ubayy did not lose his position to a rival tribal chief in a conflict he might have fought and potentially won. He lost it to a force he could not meaningfully resist or compete against — a religious and social transformation that had already reshaped the loyalties of his own city before he had any opportunity to act.

When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Medina, Abdullah ibn Ubayy made an outward profession of Islam, as did most of the city eventually. But the scholars of the Seerah, examining his subsequent conduct across the following years, consistently identify a pattern of behavior inconsistent with sincere internal conviction — behavior that aligns precisely with the signs of hypocrisy we examined in the previous lesson.

He maintained social and political influence in Medina even after his outward conversion, and at several critical moments, his actions appeared calculated to undermine the Prophet's ﷺ leadership or to position himself favorably regardless of how events unfolded.

## The Withdrawal at Uhud

The most consequential of these moments occurred as the Muslim army marched out to meet Quraysh at Uhud — an episode we will examine in full detail in the unit ahead.

Abdullah ibn Ubayy, having argued unsuccessfully for a defensive strategy within Medina rather than meeting Quraysh's forces in open battle outside the city, withdrew with approximately a third of the assembled army at a critical juncture in the march. This was not a minor defection. It meaningfully reduced the strength of the force that would go on to face Quraysh, at a moment when every available soldier mattered enormously.

The full consequences of this withdrawal, and how the remaining Muslim force responded, will be examined carefully when we reach the unit dedicated to Uhud. For now, it is enough to understand this episode as a clear demonstration of the pattern of behavior that had marked Abdullah ibn Ubayy's conduct since the Prophet's ﷺ arrival in Medina.

## Mercy Toward a Man Who Caused Harm

What makes the story of Abdullah ibn Ubayy genuinely instructive, rather than simply a chronicle of one man's treachery, is how consistently the Prophet ﷺ chose restraint over retribution.

Umar ibn al-Khattab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — whose directness and zeal for justice we have seen demonstrated repeatedly since his own dramatic conversion — urged the Prophet ﷺ on multiple occasions to have Abdullah ibn Ubayy executed for conduct that, in Umar's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> assessment, constituted clear treachery against the Muslim community.

The Prophet ﷺ refused each time. His stated reasoning combined two considerations. First, a practical concern for how such an execution would be perceived, both within Medina and beyond it — that people would say Muhammad kills his own companions, a perception that could undermine the trust and openness the Prophet ﷺ needed to maintain with the broader community. Second, and perhaps more significantly, his consistent unwillingness to act on suspicion of what resided in a person's heart rather than on clearly demonstrated and prosecutable wrongdoing.

There is also a deeply human dimension to this restraint that deserves attention. Abdullah ibn Ubayy's own son, also named Abdullah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, was among the most sincere and devoted believers in Medina. At one point, this son came to the Prophet ﷺ and offered, with complete seriousness, to personally execute his own father if the Prophet ﷺ commanded it, rather than allow anyone else to bear that burden or risk a less clean outcome. The Prophet ﷺ declined this offer as well, instructing him instead to continue treating his father with the kindness and good conduct that righteousness requires toward a parent, regardless of that parent's own failings.

## A Final Mercy

When Abdullah ibn Ubayy eventually died, his son came once more to the Prophet ﷺ, this time requesting his father's shroud be made from the Prophet's ﷺ own shirt, and asking that he lead the funeral prayer over him.

The Prophet ﷺ granted both requests — a remarkable act of mercy given everything that had preceded it, extended specifically out of consideration for the genuine grief and faith of a sincere son who had done nothing to deserve having his father's failings held against him.

Umar <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span>, present at this moment, voiced his strong objection, reminding the Prophet ﷺ of everything Abdullah ibn Ubayy had done. The Prophet ﷺ proceeded with the prayer regardless.

Following this, Allah revealed a clarifying verse:

<Quran surah="9" ayah="84" />

*And do not pray over any of them who has died, ever, or stand at his grave. Indeed, they disbelieved in Allah and His Messenger and died while they were defiantly disobedient.*

This revelation established the boundary going forward, without retroactively condemning the mercy the Prophet ﷺ had already shown. It illustrates, once again, the same pattern we observed regarding the Badr captives: the Prophet's ﷺ own inclination toward mercy, exercised sincerely, sometimes required subsequent divine clarification — and when it came, it was accepted and implemented without resistance.

## What This Story Teaches

The story of Abdullah ibn Ubayy is not primarily a story about villainy to be condemned, though his conduct caused genuine harm that this curriculum does not minimize. It is, perhaps more importantly, a study in how a Prophet entrusted with real political and military authority chose, again and again, to exercise restraint rather than the swift retribution that his position would have made entirely possible.

It also demonstrates something important for how we should think about families and communities containing both sincere and insincere members within them. A father's hypocrisy did not define or diminish his son's genuine faith, and the Prophet ﷺ took care to ensure that the son's sincerity was honored and protected throughout, even while the father's conduct received its own appropriate and eventually clarified treatment.

As we move forward into the more difficult episodes ahead — Banu Qaynuqa, Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, and eventually the painful events of Uhud where Abdullah ibn Ubayy's withdrawal had real consequences — this foundation of patient, restrained leadership, even toward those who caused genuine harm, will remain an essential context for understanding the choices the Prophet ﷺ made throughout this entire period.
