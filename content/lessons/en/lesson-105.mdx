## A Vision Six Years in the Waiting

Nearly six years had passed since the Hijrah we examined in extensive detail in Section 5 — six years that had brought the Muslim community through the trials of Badr, Uhud, the Trench, and the resolution of internal threats we have traced across every unit of this section. Throughout this entire period, Mecca had remained closed to the Muslim community, the city of their birth and the location of the Kaaba built by Ibrahim <span className="font-ar">عليه السلام</span> and Ismail <span className="font-ar">عليه السلام</span> — which we studied in such detail in Section 1 — remaining beyond their reach.

It was in this context that the Prophet ﷺ saw a dream of remarkable clarity and significance: himself entering the Masjid al-Haram peacefully, performing the rites of Umrah alongside his companions رضي الله عنهم, unopposed and unhindered.

## Preparing for Peace, Not War

Acting on this vision, the Prophet ﷺ called upon the Muslim community to join him in an expedition to Mecca — but the nature of this preparation communicated something essential about its intended purpose.

Rather than assembling an armed military force, he gathered over a thousand companions رضي الله عنهم and set out deliberately without the weapons and equipment that would characterize a campaign of war. They brought instead the sacrificial animals customary for those performing the rites of Umrah, a clear and unmistakable signal to any observer, including Quraysh's own scouts and informants, that this was a peaceful pilgrimage rather than any form of military threat.

This deliberate framing mattered considerably. Quraysh remained, at this point in the ongoing conflict we have traced throughout this section, a wary and uncertain party regarding Muslim intentions, and the Prophet ﷺ took genuine and specific care to ensure that his expressed purpose could not be reasonably mistaken for anything other than what it genuinely was.

## What This Journey Meant to the Muhajirin

For many among those who joined this expedition, particularly the muhajirin whose difficult and dangerous Hijrah from Mecca we examined in such detail across Section 5, this journey carried profound personal and emotional significance extending well beyond its religious purpose alone.

These were men and women who had left behind homes, family members, and the city of their birth under genuine threat, fleeing persecution that we traced extensively throughout Section 4. Nearly six years had passed since many of them had last seen the streets of Mecca, the Kaaba their own ancestor Ibrahim <span className="font-ar">عليه السلام</span> had built, or in some cases, family members who had remained behind and never accepted Islam.

The opportunity to return, even briefly and for the specific purpose of pilgrimage, carried a weight of longing and hope that the historical sources convey clearly through the emotional intensity with which this expedition was received and joined.

## Trust in Guidance, Prudence in Preparation

This journey exemplifies a pattern this curriculum has traced consistently since its earliest sections — a genuine and sincere trust in divine guidance, expressed here through the Prophet's ﷺ willingness to act on the vision he had received, combined always with careful and prudent human preparation rather than passive expectation that faith alone would eliminate every practical risk.

Bringing over a thousand companions رضي الله عنهم provided genuine collective strength and a measure of practical security for the journey, even as the deliberate absence of military weapons and preparation communicated clearly that this collective presence was not intended as a threat. This balance — trust in Allah's guidance combined with genuine and sensible practical wisdom — echoes precisely the pattern we examined regarding the digging of the trench in Unit 19, where innovative human effort and divine assistance worked together rather than either alone.

## Setting Out Toward Mecca

With this careful preparation complete, and with the hope and longing of the muhajirin in particular carrying genuine emotional weight alongside the broader religious purpose of the journey, the Prophet ﷺ and his more than one thousand companions رضي الله عنهم set out from Medina toward Mecca.

What awaited them, as we will examine directly in our next lesson, was not the peaceful welcome the dream had promised, but a determined and anxious Quraysh, uncertain how to respond to this unprecedented approach of a large but explicitly unarmed group of Muslims toward the very city that had persecuted and continued to oppose them. The confrontation and negotiation that followed would produce one of the most consequential and, in the immediate moment, most difficult-to-understand agreements in the entire history of early Islam.
