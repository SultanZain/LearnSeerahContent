## A New Pillar for a New Community

By the second year after the Hijrah, the Muslim community in Medina had a mosque, a constitution, a system of brotherhood, and a new direction of prayer. The structure of a functioning religious and social community was taking shape, piece by piece, exactly when each piece was needed.

Now came one of the five pillars that would define the rhythm of a Muslim's entire life: fasting.

<Quran surah="2" ayah="183" />

*O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous.*

The verse does something important in its very first clause — it places this obligation within the long history of Prophethood. Fasting was not invented for this community. It had been decreed, in various forms, upon the nations before them. What was new was its specific form: the month of Ramadan, sunrise to sunset, for an entire lunar month.

## Why a Month of Hunger

The question a thoughtful student might naturally ask is: why this particular form of worship? Why deny the body something as basic as food and water for an entire month, every year, for the rest of a believer's life?

The Quran answers directly. The purpose is taqwa.

Taqwa is one of the richest words in the Quranic vocabulary — a consciousness of Allah so present that it shapes behavior even when no one else is watching. And fasting is uniquely positioned to build this quality because of one simple fact: fasting is invisible.

A person could be fasting in a room alone, with food and water within arm's reach, and choose not to touch them — and no human being would ever know the difference between someone who broke their fast secretly and someone who did not. The only witness to the integrity of the fast is Allah.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ said, in a hadith qudsi:

> *"Every deed of the son of Adam is for him, except fasting. It is for Me, and I will reward it."*
> — Sahih al-Bukhari, 1904

A pillar of worship built entirely on private, unwitnessed sincerity. There is no pillar quite like it.

## Learning Hunger to Understand the Hungry

There is a second dimension to fasting that the Quran's broader emphasis on social justice helps illuminate.

Throughout the Quran, the believer is commanded again and again to care for the poor, to feed the hungry, to look after the orphan and the destitute. These commands are repeated with an insistence that makes clear how seriously Allah takes the obligation of a community toward its most vulnerable members.

But there is a gap that mere instruction cannot fully close: the gap between knowing about hunger and having felt it.

A person who has never gone a single day without food can be told, repeatedly, that hunger is a serious hardship — and still not fully grasp what that means in the body and the mind of someone experiencing it involuntarily, day after day, with no certainty of when it will end.

Fasting closes part of that gap. For one month each year, the wealthy feel something of what the poor feel every day of their lives — not identically, since the fasting person knows their hunger will end at sunset and has food waiting for them, but enough to build a felt empathy that pure instruction cannot replicate.

This is part of why Ramadan has always been, throughout Islamic history, a month of heightened charity. The hunger of the day naturally turns the heart toward those for whom hunger is not a choice.

## A Month Unlike Any Other

The Prophet ﷺ described the spiritual character of Ramadan in terms that mark it as categorically different from the rest of the year:

> *"When Ramadan begins, the gates of Jannah are opened, the gates of the Fire are closed, and the devils are chained."*
> — Sahih al-Bukhari, 1899

This description should not be misunderstood as a claim that wrongdoing becomes impossible during this month. People still make mistakes in Ramadan. What the hadith describes is an atmosphere — a season in which the usual obstacles to goodness are reduced, in which the soul's natural inclination toward worship is given an unusually clear path, in which the rewards for good deeds are described as multiplied.

It is, in the most literal sense, a training season. Thirty days of structured discipline — waking before dawn, restraining desire through the daylight hours, breaking the fast with gratitude at sunset, standing in extra prayer at night — designed to leave the believer changed by the time the month ends.

The Battle of Badr — which we turn to soon — took place during this very first Ramadan that the Muslim community observed. A month of fasting and a month of the first great test of the new community's survival, arriving together, in the same year, within weeks of each other.

The pattern of the Seerah holds again: worship comes first, and from worship flows the strength to face whatever comes next.
