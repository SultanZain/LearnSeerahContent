## A Community Surrounded by an Active Enemy

It would be a mistake to imagine that once the Hijrah was complete, the Muslims in Medina were simply left in peace to build their new community undisturbed.

Quraysh had not given up. The men who had surrounded the Prophet's ﷺ house with swords on the night of the Hijrah, who had offered a hundred camels for his capture, who had pursued him to the very mouth of the cave at Thawr — these were not people who considered the matter closed simply because their target had escaped to another city.

Medina sat along, and near, several of the major trade routes that connected Mecca to Syria — the same routes that Hashim had established generations earlier, that we studied in Section 2. Quraysh's caravans, carrying the wealth that sustained their economy and, by extension, their continued hostility toward the Muslim community, regularly passed within reach of Medina's territory.

The Muslims began to watch.

## Why the Caravans Mattered

It is worth understanding precisely why these caravans were not simply a neutral commercial matter that the Muslims had no legitimate interest in.

When the muhajirin fled Mecca during the Hijrah, many of them left behind homes, land, and possessions that had taken a lifetime to build. This property was not preserved for them. In several documented cases, it was seized and absorbed by Quraysh — in some instances directly incorporated into the very trading operations that funded the city's ongoing campaign against the Muslim community.

This meant the caravans were not an abstraction. They represented, in a very direct sense, the continuation of the same economic and political power that had persecuted the believers for thirteen years, that had organized the assassination plot against the Prophet ﷺ, and that showed no sign of accepting that the Muslim community in Medina had any right to exist in peace.

Monitoring these caravans was, in this light, an act of basic strategic awareness for a community that understood it remained at active risk.

## The Sariyyas

In the months following the permission to fight, the Prophet ﷺ began sending out small expeditions — known as sariyyas, distinguished from the larger ghazawat in which the Prophet ﷺ personally participated.

These early sariyyas were modest undertakings. Groups of companions رضي الله عنهم, sometimes numbering only a few dozen, were sent to observe the movement of Quraysh's caravans, to establish contact and alliances with neighboring tribes, and to make the presence of the Muslim community in the region known.

Several of these expeditions returned without any confrontation at all — their purpose fulfilled simply through reconnaissance and the establishment of relationships. They reflect a community in the careful, early stages of understanding its new position: no longer a persecuted minority hiding within a hostile city, but an organized political entity with territory, allies, and a responsibility to understand the landscape of threats and opportunities around it.

## The Expedition to Nakhlah

Among these early expeditions, the one led by Abdullah ibn Jahsh <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> to a place called Nakhlah deserves careful attention, because it became a moment of genuine controversy and required direct Quranic clarification.

Abdullah ibn Jahsh <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> and a small group of companions رضي الله عنهم encountered a small Quraysh trading caravan. An engagement took place, resulting in the death of one of the Quraysh and the capture of others along with goods from the caravan.

The difficulty was timing. The encounter occurred at a point that some understood to fall within one of the sacred months — a period during which, by the long-standing convention across Arabia that we discussed in Section 4, all fighting was traditionally prohibited. When the group returned to Medina, there was genuine uncertainty and concern, both among the Muslims and seized upon vigorously by Quraysh, about whether a sacred prohibition had been violated.

## The Quranic Response

Allah addressed the matter directly, neither dismissing the seriousness of the concern nor allowing Quraysh's accusation to stand unanswered without its proper context:

<Quran surah="2" ayah="217" />

*They ask you about the sacred month — about fighting therein. Say, "Fighting therein is great sin, but averting people from the path of Allah and disbelief in Him and obstruction of al-Masjid al-Haram and the expulsion of its people therefrom are greater in magnitude in the sight of Allah. And fitnah is greater than killing."*

This is a careful, balanced response — the kind that the Quran consistently offers when addressing complicated real situations rather than abstract hypotheticals. It does not say the sacred months no longer matter. It says fighting within them is a serious matter. But it places that seriousness in proportion alongside what Quraysh themselves had done: turning people away from belief in Allah, blocking access to the sacred mosque, and expelling an entire community from their homes simply for their faith.

The verse does not absolve any error in timing. It contextualizes it within a moral accounting that refuses to let the technical question of a calendar overshadow the deeper question of who had actually initiated injustice, and on what scale.

## A Community Learning to Navigate a New Reality

The period covered by these early sariyyas and the Nakhlah incident represents something important in the development of the Muslim community: the transition from thirteen years of pure endurance to the new and unfamiliar responsibilities of an organized political entity with its own decisions to make, its own mistakes to learn from, and its own need to balance caution with the active threats surrounding it.

The tension was rising. Quraysh's economic lifeline ran near Medina's borders. The Muslims, newly permitted to defend themselves, were learning the practical dimensions of what that permission required — intelligence, alliances, and careful judgment about when and how to act.

All of this was building toward a moment that neither side, at this point, could have fully anticipated: a caravan, a decision by Abu Sufyan to take a different route home, and a confrontation at a place called Badr that would become one of the most consequential single days in the entire history of Islam.
