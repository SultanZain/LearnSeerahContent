## Daughters of Khadijah رضي الله عنها

All four of the Prophet's ﷺ daughters who lived to adulthood — Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum, and Fatimah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنهن</span> — were born to Khadijah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span>, whose twenty-five years of marriage to the Prophet ﷺ we examined in detail across Section 3. Their lives, spanning the transformation from the difficult years of Mecca through the establishment and growth of the Muslim community in Medina, each carry their own significant story.

## Zaynab رضي الله عنها — The Eldest

Zaynab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span>, the eldest of the four daughters, married Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi, a respected merchant of Mecca who did not initially accept Islam even as his wife remained devoted to her father's message. This created a genuinely difficult circumstance of prolonged separation during the early years of the Hijrah, when Zaynab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> found herself connected by marriage to a man who had not yet embraced the faith that now defined her father's entire community.

This difficulty became especially poignant following the Battle of Badr, which we examined in extensive detail earlier in this section. Abu al-As was among those captured, and when his family in Mecca sent ransom for his release, the payment included a necklace that had once belonged to Khadijah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> — a piece of jewelry Zaynab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> had inherited from her mother and had sent to secure her husband's freedom.

When the Prophet ﷺ recognized this specific item among the ransom brought before him, the narrations describe him being visibly moved, remembering his beloved wife of twenty-five years. He asked his companions رضي الله عنهم, if they saw fit, to return the necklace and release Abu al-As without further ransom, out of consideration for Zaynab's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> circumstances — a request the companions رضي الله عنهم honored. Abu al-As eventually accepted Islam years later, and the couple was reunited.

## Ruqayyah and Umm Kulthum رضي الله عنهما — Married to Dhun-Nurayn

Ruqayyah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> married Uthman ibn Affan <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — the companion رضي الله عنه whose remarkable generosity and character we have examined throughout this curriculum, including his position among the ten companions رضي الله عنهم promised Jannah that we studied in Unit 16. Together, Ruqayyah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> and Uthman <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> were among the emigrants to Abyssinia during the persecution we examined in Section 4, and later among those who made the Hijrah to Medina.

Ruqayyah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> passed away during the period of the Battle of Badr — her illness kept Uthman <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> in Medina at the Prophet's ﷺ own instruction to care for her rather than joining the battle, addressing directly a claim sometimes raised questioning his presence at that engagement, when in fact he remained behind under explicit prophetic instruction for a matter of genuine family necessity.

Following Ruqayyah's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> death, the Prophet ﷺ arranged the marriage of his daughter Umm Kulthum <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> to Uthman <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> as well. This unique honor of having been married successively to two of the Prophet's ﷺ own daughters earned Uthman <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> the distinguished title Dhun-Nurayn — the Possessor of Two Lights — a title used and honored throughout Islamic history since.

## Fatimah رضي الله عنها — Leader of the Women of Jannah

The youngest of the four daughters, Fatimah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span>, held a position of particular closeness within the family that the narrations describe consistently. She married Ali ibn Abi Talib <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> — the companion رضي الله عنه whose acceptance of Islam as a child, whose courage in sleeping in the Prophet's ﷺ bed on the night of the Hijrah, and whose bravery in the single combat at Badr we have examined throughout this curriculum.

Together, Fatimah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> and Ali <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> became the parents of Hasan and Husayn <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنهما</span>, whose lineage would carry forward the Prophet's ﷺ own bloodline through the generations that followed. The Prophet ﷺ described his daughter with words of profound honor:

> *"Fatimah is the leader of the women of Jannah."*
> — Sahih al-Bukhari, 3624

She was the only one of the Prophet's ﷺ children to survive him, passing away approximately six months after his own death — a period we will examine in detail when we reach the concluding units of this section.

## What Their Collective Story Teaches

Considered together, the lives of these four daughters reflect a pattern this curriculum has traced consistently since its earliest lessons in Section 1: genuine faith and a position of profound honor within this history did not exempt anyone, including the Prophet's ﷺ own beloved children, from experiencing real hardship, separation, and loss alongside genuine joy and blessing.

Zaynab <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> endured years of painful separation from a husband who had not yet embraced her father's message. Ruqayyah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> experienced the hardship of the Abyssinian migration before her early death. Umm Kulthum <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span> stepped into a marriage shaped by her sister's passing. And Fatimah <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنها</span>, though held in particularly close affection, would herself face the profound grief of losing her father within a matter of months of his own death.
