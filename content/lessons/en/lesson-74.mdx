## A Decision Already Made, Then Reconsidered

As we examined in the previous lesson, the council before Uhud had concluded with the Prophet ﷺ setting aside his own initial preference for a defensive strategy and agreeing to march out and meet Quraysh's army in open battle, in deference to the strong sentiment expressed by many of his companions رضي الله عنهم, particularly the younger men eager for the opportunity Badr had denied them.

It was during this march, after the decision had already been settled and the army was underway, that Abdullah ibn Ubayy chose to act.

{/* page */}

## The Withdrawal

Abdullah ibn Ubayy approached the matter with a degree of calculated framing that deserves careful attention. He did not simply abandon the march without explanation. He presented his withdrawal as a matter of principled strategic disagreement — reminding those around him that the Prophet ﷺ had originally favored the defensive approach, and that since this preference had been set aside in favor of marching out, he and those who shared his assessment would not participate in what he characterized as an unnecessary risk.

This framing is worth noting carefully, connecting back to what we examined in the previous unit regarding the specific signs of hypocrisy the Prophet ﷺ had identified — the capacity to present genuinely harmful or self-serving action under the cover of seemingly reasonable justification. Whatever the sincerity of his stated concern about strategy, the timing and consequence of his withdrawal — occurring after the army was already in motion, at a point calculated to inflict maximum disruption rather than at the original council where such disagreement could have been resolved through legitimate debate — points toward something considerably more damaging than simple, honest dissent.

## The Scale of the Loss

The consequence of this withdrawal was significant in concrete, numerical terms. Approximately three hundred men departed alongside Abdullah ibn Ubayy, reducing the original force of roughly one thousand to approximately seven hundred men who continued forward to face Quraysh's army of around three thousand.

This meant the already considerable numerical disadvantage the Muslims faced — already outnumbered three to one even with the full original force — had now become even more severe, with the remaining army facing odds of more than four to one against the approaching Quraysh force.

## Continuing Forward

Despite this substantial reduction in their numbers, and despite the genuine discouragement that witnessing a third of one's own army depart could easily produce in those who remained, the Prophet ﷺ and the remaining force did not retreat or abandon the campaign.

They continued forward to the position at the foot of Mount Uhud, demonstrating a continued resolve that echoes the trust we examined in the council before Badr — a willingness to proceed in reliance on Allah's support even when the visible, material circumstances had become considerably less favorable than they had been at the outset.

This resolve would soon be tested directly in the battle itself, which began, as we will see in the following lesson, with genuine early success for the Muslim forces before a critical tactical decision reshaped the entire course of the engagement.

## Connecting Back to What We Already Know

This episode gains its full significance when read alongside everything we examined in the previous unit regarding Abdullah ibn Ubayy's broader history and motivations. We saw there how his resentment traced back to the kingship that had nearly been his before the arrival of Islam reshaped the political landscape of Medina entirely, and how the Prophet ﷺ had consistently chosen restraint over retribution in dealing with him despite Umar's <span className="font-ar">رضي الله عنه</span> repeated calls for harsher consequences.

This withdrawal at Uhud represents perhaps the clearest single demonstration of why that restraint, while reflecting genuine mercy and wisdom in its broader application, did not eliminate the real and material danger that a figure of Abdullah ibn Ubayy's influence and conflicted loyalty could pose to the Muslim community at a moment of critical military need.

## A Pattern the Quran Addresses Directly

The broader pattern of hypocrites withdrawing support or actively undermining the Muslim community's efforts at critical moments receives direct attention throughout the Madinan surahs, reflecting how seriously this specific danger was taken:

<Quran surah="3" ayah="167" />

*And that He might make evident those who are hypocrites. For it was said to them, "Come, fight in the way of Allah, or defend yourselves." They said, "If we had known about fighting, we would have followed you." They were nearer to disbelief that day than to belief, saying with their mouths what was not in their hearts.*

This verse, revealed in connection with the events surrounding Uhud, addresses precisely the kind of withdrawal Abdullah ibn Ubayy's group enacted — framing their departure not as honest, reasonable strategic disagreement, but as a revelation of a deeper insincerity that had been present beneath the surface profession of faith all along.

{/* page */}

## What Remained Ahead

With approximately seven hundred men now positioned at the foot of Mount Uhud, facing an army of roughly three thousand, the Prophet ﷺ organized his remaining force as carefully and strategically as the circumstances allowed.

A particular tactical decision regarding the deployment of a group of archers to a key elevated position would prove decisive in shaping how this already difficult battle unfolded — a decision, and its outcome, that we turn to directly in our next lesson.
